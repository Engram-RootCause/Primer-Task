Managing partner health means knowing what healthy is.

Problem framing

healthy  =  maximize ( order value  −  value lost  −  cost of capture )

Order value is the payment stack's input — the stack cannot increase it, only deliver it. The stack's whole job:

  • Performance — lose less order value through the funnel.
  • Cost — charge less for the passage.
Note — solved elsewhere

Minimizing value lost is a pathing problem: many possible routes to the same destination, each with its own quality and price, all changing by the minute. Other industries solved this long ago — content delivery networks steer traffic across competing vendors by measured performance, telecoms route calls the same way.

Both settled on the same rule: quality decides the route, price only breaks ties between routes that pass the quality bar.

Approach

Diagnosis

  • Traceability: every transaction's path through the payment flow — which partners touched it, at every level.

What each level does to the end margin

LevelPerformance → money inCost → money out
L1 Checkout & methodsCompletion rate · checkout latency (p95) · method coverage & fit per geoGateway fees · sales lost to slow or ill-fitting checkout
L2 3DS authenticationFrictionless share · challenge success rate3DS fees · sales lost to challenge drop-off
L3 Fraud screeningGood orders passed (1 − false-decline rate) · fraud catch ratePer-check fees · manual review cost · blocked good orders
L4 RoutingShare of traffic on the best-fit processor · retry recovery rateOrchestration fees · wasted retries (scheme reattempt fees)
L5 ProcessingAuth rate on addressable attempts · uptime · latencyMDR / interchange + markup · FX spread · downtime losses
L6 Settlement & disputesSettlement speed · dispute win rateChargebacks + fees + penalties · reconciliation effort

Non-addressable failures (insufficient funds, customer error) belong to no level and no provider — excluded from every score.

  • Sampled comparison: transaction windows scored against
    • history vs the same moment
    • own data · industry segment · all of Primer's merchants

Proposal — from score to decision

Each level offers the merchant competing options; every option gets a scorecard built from the performance and cost metrics above:

1

Knowledge. A dashboard with AI-powered summarization — per transaction, and per sample window across many transactions (cohort analysis). Powers manual investigation, and the humans using it validate the implementation itself.

2

Alerting. A notification system on top of step 1: score deviations trigger a short alert with the summary attached — getting ahead of incidents instead of finding them in the revenue numbers.

3

Recommendation. AI learns from human decisions and starts proposing its own Change / Stay — a reinforcement cycle, benchmarked on predicted vs realized outcomes.

4

Automation. Confirmation of the approach: unlocked per level as the model matches or beats a human operator's accuracy in less time.

Trade-offs

The main one: automation starves its own scorecards

Automation routes to preferred paths. Unused paths stop generating data, their scores go stale, and the system can no longer tell whether a rejected path has improved — it optimises into a local maximum and calls it healthy.

Four defences, cheapest first:

  • Network data as free exploration. A path this merchant abandoned is still running live for the rest of the Primer merchant base. Their traffic keeps its score fresh at zero cost to this merchant — the network effect makes most exploration unnecessary.
  • Confidence decays without traffic. A stale score isn't a good score. Decay turns silence into uncertainty, and uncertainty is what triggers the next two.
  • An explore budget. A small, deliberate share of traffic on alternative paths — treated as a measurable cost of keeping options honest, and spent in low-value segments first.
  • Probes where traffic can't go. Liveness and latency checks keep a dormant path's basic health observable without sending real money through it.

Other trade-offs

  • Speed vs correctness — automation is worth it only if it takes a good decision. Otherwise the human is slower BUT better.
  • Wrong attribution — one confident wrong verdict costs more trust than ten right ones earn.
  • Flapping — reacting fast enough to matter without oscillating traffic between providers.
  • Alert sensitivity — a low threshold catches small real dips but also fires on noise, and operators start ignoring alerts. A high threshold keeps every alert credible but lets slow erosion run unnoticed. Where the line sits is a decision, not a default.
  • Commercial commitments — volume deals with acquirers constrain what "optimal" is allowed to mean.
  • Thin data — small merchants and narrow corridors can't support their own scorecards; they lean on segment and fleet scopes.
  • Gaming — once providers know they're scored, some will optimise the score rather than the outcome.
