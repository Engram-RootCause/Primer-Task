Managing partner health means knowing what healthy is.

Problem framing

healthy  =  maximize ( order value  −  value lost  −  cost of capture )
north star metric: value lost per $ presented

The job

Order value is the payment stack's input — the stack cannot increase it, only deliver it. The stack's whole job:

  • Performance — lose less order value through the funnel.
  • Cost — charge less for the passage.
Out of segment insights

Minimizing value lost is a pathing problem: many routes to the same destination, each with its own quality and price, changing by the minute. CDNs and telecoms solved it long ago.

Both settled on the same rule: quality decides the route, price only breaks ties between routes that pass the quality bar.

Why it matters

  • The scale — every percentage point lost in the funnel is lost from the profit margin. Large clients feel it.
  • It's invisible — value loss in this funnel doesn't arrive as an invoice or a cost line. It's an opportunity cost merchants never feel. Add multiple partners and geo coverage, and the problem compounds.
  • On your own — as a merchant, you are on your own. As Primer, we have the data across segments to provide benchmarks.
Note — the decision is the cost

Partner health is as much about finding the best configuration as about who decides and implements the change. In large merchants that enablement is the biggest cost of the decision — lowering it is the point of orchestration.

Approach

Diagnosis

Traceability — every transaction's path through the payment flow: which partners touched it, at every level, and what each level did to the end margin. Primer's Observability already captures this per-transaction data sliced by dimension — the raw material is assumed to exist; traceability means connecting it per-path.

At L1–L5 the merchant chooses between competing providers — each choice performs and costs. Non-addressable failures (insufficient funds, customer error) belong to no level and no provider — excluded from every score.

Cohorting — transactions grouped by behaviour through the funnel. Routing knowledge isn't just partner-by-partner — it's learning which kinds of transactions belong on which path.

Sampled comparison — transaction windows scored against history vs the same moment, on own data · industry segment · all of Primer's merchants.

Proposal — from score to decision

Each level offers the merchant competing options; every option gets a scorecard built from the performance and cost metrics above:

Prioritization

1

Knowledge. Scores, traces, and cohort analysis fed into the AI Companion as data to power manual investigations — per transaction, and per sample window across many transactions. The humans investigating validate the implementation itself.

2

Alerting. A notification system on top of step 1: score deviations trigger a short alert with the summary attached — getting ahead of incidents instead of finding them in the revenue numbers.

3

Recommendation. AI learns from human decisions and starts proposing its own Change / Stay — a reinforcement cycle, benchmarked on predicted vs realized outcomes. This gets surfaced in the Workflows module as recommended workflows, rules and paths.

4

Automation. Confirmation of the approach: unlocked per level as the model matches or beats a human operator's accuracy in less time.

Trade-offs

The main one: automation starves its own scorecards

Automation routes to preferred paths. Unused paths stop generating data, their scores go stale, and the system can't tell whether a rejected path improved — it optimises into a local maximum and calls it healthy.

Four defences, cheapest first:

  • Network data as free exploration. A path this merchant abandoned still runs live for other Primer merchants — their traffic keeps its score fresh at no cost here.
  • Confidence decays without traffic. Silence becomes uncertainty, and uncertainty triggers the next two.
  • An explore budget. A small deliberate share of traffic on alternatives — a measurable cost of keeping options honest, spent in low-value segments first.
  • Probes where traffic can't go. Liveness and latency checks keep a dormant path observable without sending real money through it.

Other trade-offs

  • Speed vs correctness — automation is worth it only if it takes a good decision. Otherwise the human is slower BUT better.
  • Wrong attribution — one confident wrong verdict costs more trust than ten right ones earn.
  • Flapping — reacting fast enough to matter without oscillating traffic between providers.
  • Alert sensitivity — a low threshold catches small real dips but also fires on noise, and operators start ignoring alerts. A high threshold keeps every alert credible but lets slow erosion run unnoticed. Where the line sits is a decision, not a default.
  • Commercial commitments — volume deals with acquirers constrain what "optimal" is allowed to mean.
  • Thin data — small merchants and narrow corridors can't support their own scorecards; they lean on segment and network scopes.
  • Gaming — once providers know they're scored, some will optimise the score rather than the outcome.

Assumptions and open questions

Assumed

  • We can follow each transaction through every level of the flow.
  • We know what every provider charges, well enough to calculate real costs.
  • We're allowed to use data from all merchants for comparisons.
  • We have enough past incidents to test the scoring against before showing it to anyone.
  • Merchants have someone who will actually read the alerts and act.

Open

  • When comparing a merchant to "similar merchants" — similar how? Industry, size, geography?
  • When everything dips at once, how do we tell "this partner has a problem" from "the whole market has a problem"?
  • Should partners see the scores we give them?
  • How small can a merchant be before their own data is too thin to score honestly?
  • Is this part of the product, or something merchants pay extra for?
